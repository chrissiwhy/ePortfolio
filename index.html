01 — ABOUT ME

My journey into civil engineering

I'm a third-year Civil Engineering student at UTS, maintaining a WAM of 76.62 and High Distinctions across five subjects, driven by a simple belief: that good infrastructure changes people's everyday lives, and I want to be part of building it. Site-based construction work is what appeals to me most, seeing how a design becomes something tangible and having a direct hand in delivering it safely and well. That said, I also see value in gaining some experience in design engineering along the way. During my internship at Australian Piling Group, I got my first real taste of this, supporting site inspections and verifying piling works against engineering drawings, and it confirmed that this is exactly the kind of work I want to build a career around. During one inspection, I noticed a pile position on site didn't quite match what the engineering drawing showed. Rather than assume the drawing was right, I flagged it to my supervisor before the pile was installed, and it turned out the site measurement was correct and the drawing needed updating. My goal is to move into a graduate civil engineering role with a construction contractor, where I can keep developing that site-based, delivery-focused experience.

Alongside that internship, I completed an individual road design project that was commended by my subject tutor for its rigour. It was my first real experience of engineering design from brief to finished report. Beyond the technical side, I care about work that genuinely benefits the people who use it, whether that's a road, a bridge, or a piece of public infrastructure. I'm currently looking for opportunities that combine strong technical civil engineering work with real, meaningful impact on the communities it serves.

07 — REFLECTION

On my internship experience

Piling rig and crawler crane on site during my internship at Australian Piling Group.

What were your expectations about your internship before you joined?

Before starting I expected the role to be largely observational. I pictured shadowing engineers, learning terminology and staying on the sidelines of a fast-moving construction site. I assumed most of my time would be spent in an office setting reviewing drawings and specifications with limited hands-on involvement in what was happening on the ground. This expectation reflected the way engineering is often taught at university, where technical knowledge is presented as something to be mastered before it is applied, rather than developed through direct practice.

What was the reality? How was it different from your expectations?

The reality was different. From early on I was directly involved in site inspections, monitoring piling works and verifying construction activities matched engineering drawings and project specifications. Rather than passively observing I was expected to interpret pile layouts and documentation myself and use that understanding to support site measurements and progress tracking. The pace of a live construction site also surprised me. Decisions and coordination between engineers, supervisors and subcontractors happened quickly and clear communication mattered as much as technical accuracy. I quickly learned that being useful on site meant asking the right questions early rather than waiting until I fully understood every detail. This gap between what I expected and what the role required reflects what Schön (1983) describes as the difference between technical rationality, applying learned theory directly to a problem, and reflection-in-action, where professionals think on their feet and adjust their understanding as a situation unfolds. During one inspection, I noticed a pile position didn't quite match the engineering drawing. Rather than assume the drawing was correct, I raised it with my supervisor before the pile went in, and it turned out the site measurement was right and the drawing needed updating.

What lessons were the most important from your internship? Why were they important?

The most important lesson was how differently design documentation reads once you are standing on the site it describes. In my coursework, particularly my surveying subject, I had worked with plans, chainages and levels largely on paper. Seeing how those concepts translated into real piling equipment, ground conditions and construction sequencing gave me a deeper understanding of why precision in documentation matters, since small discrepancies between drawings and site conditions have real, immediate consequences. This reframed engineering drawings for me as a working tool construction teams rely on daily, not just an academic exercise. This progression, from abstract concept, to concrete site experience, to a revised understanding I can apply going forward, closely mirrors Kolb's (1984) experiential learning cycle, which describes learning as a continuous process of experiencing, reflecting, conceptualising and testing new understanding in practice.

A second key lesson was the value of clear, professional communication across a multidisciplinary team. Supporting engineers, supervisors and subcontractors meant communicating findings clearly and asking questions when documentation and site conditions did not align. This skill felt as important as any technical knowledge I brought to the role. Trevelyan (2014) argues that engineering practice is fundamentally a social and collaborative activity rather than a purely technical one, and that much of an engineer's real work involves coordinating with others rather than performing calculations alone. My experience on site reflected this directly, since the value of my technical observations depended on how clearly I could communicate them to the people who needed to act on them.

After your workplace experience, what would you say your value proposition would be to an employer? How can you demonstrate this?

I would describe my value proposition as someone who can bridge technical documentation and practical site reality. I can read and interpret engineering drawings accurately and then apply that understanding on the ground through careful measurement, verification and reporting. I can demonstrate this through my experience supporting piling inspections at Australian Piling Group as well as my coursework achievements in Surveying, Soil Behaviour and Mechanics of Solids, which underpin the technical judgment this work requires. My background in customer-facing roles also means I can communicate clearly with people across different roles and levels of technical knowledge, a skill I saw was genuinely necessary on-site and not just client-facing, consistent with Trevelyan's (2014) view that communication is central to engineering competence rather than secondary to it. Together these experiences show an employer I can move comfortably between technical and interpersonal demands, a large part of what civil engineering site work requires.

How did your internship influence the type of role in which you are interested?

This internship has shaped my interest toward site-based and construction-focused civil engineering roles rather than purely design-office work. I found real satisfaction in seeing how design translates into physical construction and I am now more drawn to roles that combine technical engineering knowledge with active, on-the-ground problem-solving. The experience also sharpened my awareness of safety: verifying that construction activities genuinely matched engineering drawings was not just a documentation exercise, since a missed discrepancy on site could compromise the safety of the works themselves. Testing my understanding directly against real conditions, rather than relying on what I had learned in theory alone, is how I learn most effectively, and that will shape how I approach future professional development. Going forward I am interested in seeking a graduate role with a construction contractor delivering large-scale civil infrastructure projects, to build further on the site-based experience I gained here.

Kolb, D. A. (1984). Experiential learning: Experience as the source of learning and development. Prentice-Hall.

Schön, D. A. (1983). The reflective practitioner: How professionals think in action. Basic Books.

My internship at Australian Piling Group and my involvement in a range of engineering design projects and customer-facing roles have prepared me to contribute effectively to this environment. I address the selection criteria below.

A commitment to ethical conduct and the highest standards of professional accountability

During my internship at Australian Piling Group, I was responsible for verifying construction activities against engineering drawings and project specifications, work that required strict accuracy and honesty in reporting, since discrepancies could compromise safety or project outcomes. During one inspection, I noticed a pile position on site didn't quite match what the engineering drawing showed. Rather than assume the drawing was correct and move on, I flagged it to my supervisor before the pile was installed. It turned out the site measurement was right and the drawing needed updating, so the issue was dealt with before the pile went in. That experience showed me that professional accountability means raising an issue even when it would be easier to assume everything is fine, not just following procedure when nothing is wrong.

Demonstrated ability to effectively communicate both with other engineers and with stakeholders from different fields

At Matrix Education, parents would sometimes become frustrated when their child missed a lesson due to illness or another commitment, since Matrix doesn't offer make-up classes for missed sessions. Rather than simply repeating the policy, I explained clearly why make-up lessons weren't available and worked through the alternatives we do offer. I helped the parent access their child's student portal, confirming their login so they could watch a recording of the missed lesson, and gave them clear steps to book a one-on-one workshop themselves in future, in person or online with a Matrix tutor, so they had a way to handle it independently next time. I focused on what their child could still access rather than on what Matrix couldn't offer, and the parent left knowing how to watch the missed lesson and how to book support themselves. This experience taught me that communicating well with stakeholders from different fields is not just about being clear, it is about translating a limitation into a workable path forward, a skill I also relied on when communicating with engineers and subcontractors on site at Australian Piling Group.

The ability to engage with a creative, innovative and proactive environment

At Matrix Education, parents often heard about the online lesson videos and complimentary one-on-one workshops only after their child had missed a lesson. Rather than waiting for that to happen, I made a point of advising parents at enrolment about what their child's course included: access to every lesson as an online video, and a complimentary one-on-one workshop with a Matrix tutor. This meant parents already knew where to turn if a lesson was missed. Taking that step without being asked showed me that being proactive often means giving people the information before they need it, not just responding well once a problem has come up.

Demonstrated ability to use and manage information

On one occasion I needed to reconcile a pile position shown on the engineering drawing with what the site survey data actually showed, since the two did not match. Rather than treating the drawing as the automatic source of truth, I cross-checked it against the recorded site measurements, confirmed the discrepancy was real, and reported it to my supervisor. The site measurement was correct and the drawing needed updating. This experience showed me that managing information well is not just about reading and organising it, it is about knowing when to question a source and verify it against other data before treating it as fact.

The ability to manage your own performance in a professional environment

At Matrix Education, holiday periods, especially the first day back, brought a sudden spike in demands all at once: teacher role issues, parents walking in, and new enrolments all needing attention simultaneously, with no way to handle everything in order of arrival. Rather than working through tasks as they came in, I prioritised whatever was most time-sensitive first, generally teacher or class-related issues, before moving on to walk-ins and new enrolments. That kept the most urgent problems from waiting behind less pressing tasks. Managing my own performance in that environment meant constantly reassessing priority under pressure, not just working through a fixed list.

A demonstrated ability to work as part of a team and to show leadership when required

At Australian Piling Group, I collaborated with engineers, supervisors, and subcontractors to support the safe and efficient delivery of civil construction works. On one occasion, two piling teams both needed a verification check around the same time and my supervisor was not immediately available. Rather than wait, I made the call to check the pile that was about to proceed with installation first, since an issue there would be harder to correct once construction moved past that point, and let the second team know there would be a short delay. When my supervisor returned, I explained the reasoning behind the order I had chosen. That experience taught me that leadership on site often means making a reasonable call under time pressure and being able to justify it afterward, not waiting for direction on every decision.

I would welcome the opportunity to bring my technical skills, professional accountability, and enthusiasm for hands-on engineering to the Industrus Engineering Graduate Program. Thank you for considering my application. I look forward to the opportunity to discuss my suitability for this role further.
